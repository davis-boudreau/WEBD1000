# 1. Workshop Details & Overview

## Purpose

In Workshop 01, you evaluated existing websites to understand how usability, accessibility, navigation, and visual design influence the user experience.

In Workshop 02, you become the designer.

You will begin planning your own Semester Website Project using **Figma**. Rather than immediately writing HTML and CSS, you will investigate your website's purpose, identify its users, organize content, develop its navigation, and create preliminary page layouts.

Your goal is to create a website blueprint that can guide development in later workshops.

### Why This Matters

Website development is not simply writing code.

A successful website requires decisions about:

- Who will use the website.
- What visitors need to accomplish.
- Which pages and content are required.
- How users will navigate between pages.
- How information should be arranged.
- How the layout will adapt to different screen sizes.

Making these decisions early reduces confusion, unnecessary coding, and redesign work.

### Workshop Challenge

**Plan a functional, multi-page website that helps its intended audience find information and complete important tasks.**

All workshop artifacts must be created and maintained in **one Figma Design file**.

# 4. Concepts & Theory — Website Planning

## 4.1 What Is Website Planning?

Website planning is the process of defining what a website must accomplish and determining how its content and interface should be organized.

A simple planning process is:

**Purpose → Audience → Requirements → Content → Sitemap → Navigation → Wireframes**

### Example: Community Recreation Website

Imagine a community recreation centre needs a website.

Its purpose is to help residents discover programs and register for activities.

Its audience includes parents, students, seniors, and other residents.

Important user goals might include:

- Find available programs.
- View schedules.
- Locate the recreation centre.
- Find contact information.

These goals help determine the website's content and pages.

### 💡 Think About It

Would you design the same homepage for a community recreation centre and a photographer's portfolio?

Why or why not?

### Why This Matters

A website should be designed around user needs rather than the developer's personal preferences.

### Embedded Theory — User Goals

A user goal describes something a visitor wants to accomplish.

A useful format is:

**As a [type of user], I want to [action], so that [benefit].**

Example:

This statement identifies the user, their intended action, and why it matters.

### What You Will Do

### Why This Matters

Content should influence website structure. Designing pages without considering their content can lead to missing information and confusing layouts.

### Embedded Theory — Content Inventory

A content inventory is a structured list of information required by a website.

For example:

| Content | Purpose | Page | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening hours | Help users plan a visit | Contact | High |

Priority indicates how important the information is to the user's goals.

## Activity D — Design Your Information Architecture and Sitemap

**Estimated Time:** 30 minutes

### What You Will Do

Create a visual diagram of your website's pages and their relationships.

### Why This Matters

Information architecture helps visitors understand where information belongs and how to find it.

### Embedded Theory — Information Architecture

**Information architecture (IA)** is the organization and structure of information.

For websites, IA helps answer:

- What pages are needed?
- How should content be grouped?
- Which pages belong in primary navigation?
- How will visitors find information?

A **sitemap** is a visual representation of that structure.

Example:

```text
                     HOME
                       |
            +----------+----------+
            |          |          |
          ABOUT      PROGRAMS    CONTACT
            |          |
          HISTORY    SCHEDULE
```

The sitemap describes relationships between pages.

It does not describe the layout of individual pages.

### Task D1 — Create Your Sitemap

On Page 03:

1. Create a rectangle labelled **Home**.
2. Add at least three additional website pages.
3. Use connecting lines to show relationships.
4. Label every page clearly.
5. Group related pages logically.

Your sitemap must contain **at least four pages, including Home**.

### Task D2 — Analyze Your Sitemap

Identify which pages support your three user goals.

Add a short explanation directly beside your sitemap.

### 💡 Try This

Pretend you are a first-time visitor.

Starting at Home, trace the path to your most important information.

Does the structure make sense?

## Activity E — Plan Website Navigation

**Estimated Time:** 20 minutes

### What You Will Do

Develop the navigation structure that visitors will use to move between pages.

### Why This Matters

Navigation should be predictable, understandable, and consistent.

### Embedded Theory — Navigation

Navigation is the collection of links and controls used to move around a website.

Good navigation uses:

- Clear labels.
- Consistent placement.
- Logical grouping.
- Predictable destinations.
- Access to important information.

Example:

```text

+-------------------------------------------+
```

## Activity F — Create Low-Fidelity Wireframes

**Estimated Time:** 60 minutes

### What You Will Do

Design preliminary layouts for desktop and mobile screens.

### Why This Matters

Wireframes help developers solve layout and content problems before writing code.

### Embedded Theory — Wireframes

A wireframe is a simplified representation of a webpage.

It identifies where content and interface elements belong.

A wireframe generally focuses on:

- Header.
- Navigation.
- Hero section.
- Main content.
- Calls to action.
- Footer.
- Visual hierarchy.

A low-fidelity wireframe uses simple shapes and labels rather than polished graphics.

### Example Homepage Wireframe

```text
+--------------------------------------+
| LOGO            HOME ABOUT CONTACT   |
+--------------------------------------+
|                                      |
|               HERO                   |
|          Main Heading                |
|          Supporting Text             |
|          [ Call to Action ]          |
|                                      |
+--------------------------------------+
|            MAIN CONTENT              |
|                                      |
|       [ ITEM ] [ ITEM ] [ ITEM ]     |
|                                      |
+--------------------------------------+
|               FOOTER                 |
+--------------------------------------+
```

### Embedded Theory — Visual Hierarchy

Visual hierarchy helps visitors understand which information is most important.

Designers establish hierarchy through:

- Size.
- Position.
- Spacing.
- Grouping.
- Contrast.
- Repetition.

For example, a homepage heading should generally be more visually prominent than supporting paragraph text.

### Task F1 — Desktop Homepage

On Page 05, create a desktop frame approximately **1440 pixels wide**.

Include:

1. Header.
2. Logo or website-name placeholder.
3. Navigation.
4. Hero section.
5. Main heading.
6. Supporting text.
7. Call-to-action element.
8. Main content sections.
9. Footer.

### Task F2 — Desktop Secondary Page

Create a second desktop frame.

Choose a secondary page from your sitemap.

Include:

- Consistent header and navigation.
- Page heading.
- Relevant content.
- Consistent footer.

### Task F3 — Mobile Homepage

Create a mobile frame approximately **390 pixels wide**.

Adapt your homepage layout.

Consider:

- Stacking content vertically.
- Reducing multi-column layouts.
- Preserving the primary action.
- Adjusting navigation.
- Maintaining readable text and appropriate spacing.

### Embedded Theory — Responsive Planning

Responsive design allows a website's layout to adapt to different screen sizes.

A desktop layout might place three cards in one row.

A mobile layout might stack the same cards vertically.

**Responsive design is not simply shrinking the desktop page.** It involves reorganizing the layout so information remains usable.

### Task F4 — Annotate Your Wireframes

Add at least four short annotations explaining decisions about:

- Visual hierarchy.
- Navigation.
- Content grouping.
- Desktop/mobile differences.

### 💡 Try This

Auto Layout can help organize repeated items and control spacing, but mastering it is not required for this workshop.

## Activity G — Evaluate, Test and Improve

**Estimated Time:** 25 minutes

### What You Will Do

Review your design using principles introduced in Workshop 01.

### Why This Matters

Testing a design before implementation can reveal problems that would otherwise require code changes.

### Embedded Theory — Design Evaluation

The CRAP principles provide a useful starting point:

| Principle | Evaluation Question |
|---|---|
| Contrast | Is important information visually distinguishable? |
| Repetition | Are repeated interface elements consistent? |
| Alignment | Are elements arranged deliberately? |
| Proximity | Is related information grouped together? |

Also consider usability and accessibility:

- Are labels understandable?
- Can visitors identify important actions?
- Is content organized logically?
- Does the mobile layout preserve essential information?
- Is the planned heading structure meaningful?

### Task G1 — Conduct a Self-Review

Review your sitemap, navigation, and wireframes.

Identify three opportunities for improvement.

### Task G2 — Conduct a Peer Review

Ask another student to inspect your Figma design.

Without explaining where everything is located, ask them to:

1. Identify the website's purpose.
2. Find the page containing important information.
3. Identify the main homepage action.

Record two observations from the review.

### Task G3 — Make Improvements

Record three improvements using:

**Observation → Principle → User Impact → Improvement**

Example:

*Observation:* The main action is difficult to identify.

*Principle:* Visual hierarchy.

*User Impact:* Visitors may overlook the primary action.

*Improvement:* Move the action into the hero section and make its placeholder more prominent.

Update your Figma designs accordingly.

### Checkpoint G

- [ ] I reviewed my design using CRAP principles.
- [ ] I considered usability and accessibility.
- [ ] I collected peer feedback.
- [ ] I recorded three improvements.
- [ ] I updated my Figma design.

**Worksheet Question G:** Which improvement had the greatest impact on your design, and why?
