# WEBD 3100 — Web Design Fundamentals

## Topic 1 — Course Tooling & Web Design Foundations

### Introduction

Welcome to WEBD 3100 — Web Design Fundamentals.

In this course, you will move beyond simply asking:

**“How do I build a website?”**

Instead, we will begin asking:

**“How do I design an effective web experience, and how do I turn that design into a professional implementation?”**

You already have experience developing websites. WEBD 3100 builds on those skills by focusing on the decisions that happen **before, during, and after implementation**.

Throughout the semester, you will examine how purpose, audience, usability, accessibility, visual communication, content, and technical implementation work together to create effective web experiences.

You will then apply these ideas through a semester-long Website Redesign Project.

---

# 1. What Is Web Design?

Web design is more than choosing colours, fonts, images, and attractive layouts.

A web interface exists to help people accomplish something.

A visitor might need to:

* find information;
* purchase a product;
* register for a service;
* complete a form;
* contact an organization;
* compare options;
* learn how to perform a task;
* access an application; or
* navigate through information.

The design of the website should help the user accomplish that goal.

Good web design therefore requires us to consider several connected factors:

**Purpose → Audience → Content → Usability → Accessibility → Visual Communication → Technical Execution**

A visually attractive website is not necessarily a well-designed website.

Likewise, a technically sophisticated website may still provide a poor user experience.

Effective web design brings these areas together.

---

# 2. Purpose

Every website should have a reason for existing.

Before designing an interface, we should be able to answer:

**What is this website trying to accomplish?**

Consider the difference between:

* an online store;
* a college website;
* a government service;
* a news website;
* a restaurant website;
* a portfolio;
* a banking application; and
* a social media platform.

Each has a different purpose.

The purpose influences almost every design decision that follows.

For example, the most important action on an online store might be:

**Add to Cart**

On a college website, it might be:

**Apply Now**

On a restaurant website, it might be:

**View Menu** or **Reserve a Table**

Design should help users recognize and complete important actions.

### Questions to Ask

When examining a website, ask:

* What is the primary purpose of this site?
* What does the organization want the visitor to do?
* What does the visitor want to accomplish?
* Are those goals obvious?
* Does the design support those goals?

---

# 3. Audience

A website is designed for people.

Understanding those people is an important part of web design.

A website intended for experienced software developers may communicate very differently from a website designed for people applying for government benefits.

Consider:

* who the users are;
* what they already know;
* why they are visiting;
* what devices they may use;
* what information they need;
* what barriers they may encounter; and
* what they expect from similar websites.

A designer should avoid designing only for themselves.

A design that makes sense to the designer may not make sense to the intended user.

This leads to an important principle for this course:

> **Design decisions should be supported by user needs and evidence—not simply personal preference.**

---

# 4. Content

Users normally visit websites because they want **information or functionality**.

Content may include:

* text;
* images;
* video;
* navigation;
* forms;
* products;
* documentation;
* instructions;
* data; or
* interactive services.

Good design helps users determine:

**Where am I?**

**What is available here?**

**What is important?**

**What can I do?**

**Where should I go next?**

Content therefore needs structure.

Headings, navigation, grouping, spacing, typography, imagery, and layout all help communicate relationships between pieces of information.

Later in the course, we will explore this more formally through **information architecture, sitemaps, content hierarchy, and wireframing**.

---

# 5. Usability

Usability considers how effectively people can use an interface.

A website might look impressive but become frustrating if users cannot determine how to accomplish basic tasks.

Consider a user trying to find a company's contact information.

If the information exists but takes ten minutes to locate, the website technically contains the information—but the experience is not particularly usable.

Usability asks questions such as:

* Can users understand the interface?
* Can they find what they need?
* Is navigation predictable?
* Are important actions obvious?
* Does the interface provide useful feedback?
* Can users recover from mistakes?
* Are unnecessary steps creating friction?
* Does the interface behave in ways users expect?

In Topic 2, we will introduce **Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics**, which provide a more formal framework for evaluating many of these questions.

For now, begin thinking about usability from the user's perspective:

> **Can the user accomplish what they came here to do?**

---

# 6. Accessibility

The Web should be usable by people with a wide range of abilities, technologies, and circumstances.

Accessibility needs to be considered throughout design and development rather than added only after a website has been completed.

Examples include:

* sufficient colour contrast;
* readable text;
* keyboard-accessible interfaces;
* visible focus indicators;
* meaningful alternative text for images;
* properly labelled forms;
* logical heading structures;
* semantic HTML; and
* interfaces that do not depend exclusively on colour, sound, or visual position to communicate meaning.

Accessibility is both a **design concern and an implementation concern**.

Later in the course, you will perform accessibility testing and remediation on implemented interfaces.

---

# 7. Visual Communication

Visual design is not simply decoration.

Design communicates.

Consider what happens when you first open a webpage.

Before reading every word, you probably notice certain things:

* a large heading;
* an image;
* a navigation bar;
* a button;
* an unusual colour;
* an announcement;
* a large block of whitespace; or
* an element that visually dominates the page.

These relationships influence where users look and what they believe is important.

Visual design can help communicate:

**importance, relationships, grouping, hierarchy, actions, identity, and meaning.**

In upcoming topics, we will examine visual communication through:

* visual hierarchy;
* balance;
* alignment;
* proximity;
* repetition;
* contrast;
* whitespace;
* colour;
* typography; and
* the CRAP design principles.

The important idea for Topic 1 is:

> **Visual choices should have a purpose.**

---

# 8. Technical Execution

Eventually, a design has to work.

A beautiful Figma design is not the final website.

The implemented interface must accurately translate the intended design into a responsive, accessible, usable web experience.

This includes considerations such as:

* semantic HTML;
* maintainable CSS;
* responsive layouts;
* navigation;
* images and media;
* accessibility;
* browser compatibility;
* performance;
* working links and functionality; and
* consistent behaviour across devices.

WEBD 3100 therefore connects **design decisions with implementation decisions**.

You will not design something in Figma and then forget about it.

You will be expected to turn that design into a working web experience.

---

# 9. Web Design vs. Web Development

Web design and web development overlap, but they are not identical.

**Web design** asks questions such as:

* Who are the users?
* What are they trying to accomplish?
* What content is most important?
* How should information be organized?
* What should users notice first?
* How should navigation work?
* What should the interface look and feel like?
* Is the interface understandable and usable?

**Web development** asks questions such as:

* How should the interface be structured in HTML?
* How should the layout be implemented?
* How should responsive behaviour work?
* How should reusable components be constructed?
* How should the code be organized and maintained?
* How should the website be deployed?

Professional web work requires these areas to connect.

In WEBD 3100, we will continually move between them.

# 10. The WEBD 3100 Design-to-Production Workflow

Three core technologies support the course workflow:

## Figma — Design

Figma will be used for activities such as:

* visual exploration;
* style guides;
* design systems;
* wireframes;
* responsive interface designs;
* reusable components;
* prototypes; and
* design critique.

Your Figma files provide evidence of **what you designed and how the design evolved**.

## Git and GitHub — Develop and Version

Git and GitHub will support the implementation of your designs.

They provide:

* version control;
* repositories;
* development history;
* commits;
* project organization; and
* evidence of implementation progress.

Your repository provides evidence of **how the website was developed and managed**.

## GitHub Pages — Deploy

GitHub Pages will be used to publish working web projects.

This gives you a deployed website that can be:

* viewed in a browser;
* tested;
* shared;
* evaluated for usability;
* evaluated for accessibility;
* compared with the original design; and
* included in a portfolio.

GitHub Pages provides evidence of **what the user actually experiences**.

Together, these create our core workflow:

**Figma → Git/GitHub → GitHub Pages**

Or, more broadly:

**Design → Develop → Version → Deploy → Verify**

# 12. The Website Redesign Project

A semester-long **Website Redesign Project** provides the main context for applying your learning.

Rather than waiting until the end of the semester to begin a large final project, you will progressively develop the project through several stages.

You will establish a design direction, organize the content, create wireframes, develop high-fidelity interfaces, build an interactive prototype, implement the website, publish it, evaluate it, and refine it.

Your final project should therefore show more than a finished website.

It should demonstrate a **design process**.

You should increasingly be able to explain:

> Here was the requirement.

> Here was the design decision I made.

> Here is how I represented that decision in Figma.

> Here is how I implemented it.

> Here is what testing revealed.

> Here is what I changed as a result.

That ability to explain and justify your work is an important part of professional design practice.

# 16. Topic 1 — Website Design Critique

An important activity in Topic 1 is examining existing websites.

When looking at a website, avoid immediately asking:

**“Do I like it?”**

Instead, begin asking:

### Purpose

What is this website trying to accomplish?

### Audience

Who appears to be the intended user?

### Content

Is important information easy to identify and understand?

### Usability

Can users accomplish the tasks they are likely to perform?

### Accessibility

Are there obvious barriers that could make the interface difficult for some users?

### Visual Communication

What attracts attention first? Does that match what is actually important?

### Technical Experience

Does the website behave effectively across the device and browser being used?

The objective is to move from **personal opinion toward reasoned analysis**.

# Topic 1 Key Takeaways

By the end of Topic 1, you should understand that:

* web design is more than visual appearance;
* effective websites are designed around purpose and audience;
* content needs organization and hierarchy;
* usability considers whether people can accomplish their goals;
* accessibility needs to be considered throughout design and development;
* visual design communicates meaning and importance;
* designs eventually need to become working implementations;
* Figma, Git/GitHub, and GitHub Pages support the course's design-to-production workflow;
* critique should be supported by reasoning rather than personal preference;
* workshops involve discussion, collaboration, application, feedback, and reflection; and
* the semester project represents a continuous design and development process rather than an isolated final assignment.
