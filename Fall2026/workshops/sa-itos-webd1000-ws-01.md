# Concept Review / Lesson Content

## 1. A Website Is Built for a User

Websites aren't simply collections of HTML, CSS, images, and links.

They exist to help people accomplish something.

A user might visit a website to:

* find information;
* purchase a product;
* register for a service;
* contact an organization;
* complete a form;
* read an article;
* watch content;
* locate a business;
* perform a task.

This means an important question when evaluating a website is:

> **Can the intended user accomplish what they came here to do?**

A visually impressive website can still be difficult to use.

A technically functioning website can still be inaccessible.

A simple website can be extremely effective.

---

# 2. Purpose and Audience

Before judging a website, determine what the website is trying to accomplish.

Ask:

* What is this website for?
* Who appears to be its intended audience?
* What are users likely trying to accomplish?
* What information or actions appear most important?

For example, imagine a restaurant website.

A visitor might want to:

* view the menu;
* check opening hours;
* find the location;
* make a reservation;
* contact the restaurant.

If those common tasks are difficult to complete, the website may have a usability problem even if it looks attractive.

---

# 3. Usability

**Usability** describes how effectively, efficiently, and comfortably users can interact with a website to accomplish their goals.

When evaluating usability, consider questions such as:

### Navigation

* Is navigation easy to locate?
* Are navigation labels understandable?
* Can users predict where a link will take them?
* Is navigation consistent across pages?

### Content

* Is important information easy to find?
* Are headings meaningful?
* Is the page easy to scan?
* Are paragraphs manageable?

### Interaction

* Are buttons recognizable?
* Are links distinguishable?
* Are Calls to Action clear?
* Does the interface provide useful feedback?

### Consistency

* Do similar elements behave similarly?
* Does navigation remain consistent?
* Are visual patterns reused?

### Efficiency

* How much effort does a user need to complete a common task?
* Are unnecessary steps creating friction?

Usability focuses on the **experience of accomplishing a task**.

---

# 4. Accessibility

**Web accessibility** means designing and developing web content so that people with a wide range of abilities can perceive, understand, navigate, and interact with it.

Accessibility should not be treated as something added to a website at the end.

It is part of good web development.

Some users may:

* use a keyboard instead of a mouse;
* use a screen reader;
* have limited vision;
* have colour-vision differences;
* magnify page content;
* have difficulty with small interactive targets;
* require captions or text alternatives;
* interact with the site using assistive technology.

When performing an introductory accessibility evaluation, consider:

* Is text readable?
* Is there sufficient visual contrast?
* Can links be distinguished?
* Do images appear to communicate information that would require text alternatives?
* Are headings organized meaningfully?
* Can interactive elements be reached using a keyboard?
* Does the interface depend only on colour to communicate meaning?
* Does the page remain understandable when zoomed?

Later in the course, you'll perform more systematic accessibility testing.

For now, the goal is to begin thinking beyond your own experience as a user.

# 5. Visual Design Supports Communication

Visual design isn't simply decoration.

Design can help users understand:

* what is important;
* what belongs together;
* what is interactive;
* where to look next;
* how information is organized.

Four useful principles for evaluating visual design are:

**Contrast → Repetition → Alignment → Proximity**

These are often remembered as **CRAP**.

---

## Contrast

Contrast creates visual differences between elements.

Contrast can involve:

* size;
* weight;
* colour;
* shape;
* spacing;
* typography.

Strong contrast can help users distinguish:

```text
PAGE TITLE
    ↓
SECTION HEADING
    ↓
BODY CONTENT
    ↓
SECONDARY INFORMATION
```

Ask:

> Can the user quickly determine what is most important?

Contrast must also support readability. Text that blends into its background may look subtle but be difficult to read.

---

## Repetition

Repetition creates consistency.

Websites commonly repeat:

* colours;
* fonts;
* button styles;
* navigation patterns;
* spacing;
* card designs;
* heading styles.

Repetition helps users learn the interface.

If every button looks different, users must repeatedly determine what is clickable.

Ask:

> Are similar things presented in similar ways?

---

## Alignment

Alignment creates visual relationships between elements.

Poor alignment can make a page feel disorganized even when the user cannot immediately explain why.

Look for relationships among:

* headings;
* paragraphs;
* images;
* cards;
* navigation;
* buttons;
* page margins.

Ask:

> Do elements appear intentionally positioned in relation to one another?

---

## Proximity

Proximity helps communicate which elements belong together.

Items that are related should generally appear visually connected.

For example:

```text
PRODUCT NAME
Product description
Price
[Buy Now]
```

If the Buy Now button appears much closer to another product, the interface becomes ambiguous.

Ask:

> Can users tell which pieces of information belong together?

---

# 6. Visual Hierarchy

Visual hierarchy helps users understand the relative importance of information.

A webpage may use:

* size;
* position;
* typography;
* whitespace;
* contrast;
* grouping;

to establish hierarchy.

A clear hierarchy might communicate:

```text
SITE
  ↓
PAGE
  ↓
SECTION
  ↓
CONTENT
  ↓
ACTION
```

Users rarely read every webpage from beginning to end.

They frequently **scan**.

Good hierarchy supports that behaviour.

---

# 7. Navigation

Navigation provides a map through the website.

Effective navigation should help users answer questions such as:

* Where am I?
* What sections exist?
* Where can I go?
* How do I return?
* What will happen when I select this link?

When evaluating navigation, examine:

* location;
* visibility;
* wording;
* consistency;
* number of choices;
* organization;
* current-page indication;
* mobile behaviour where applicable.

---

# 8. Evidence-Based Evaluation

One of the most important skills in this workshop is learning to separate **preference** from **evaluation**.

Consider:

> "I don't like the colours."

This tells us something about the evaluator's preference, but very little about the website.

Compare it with:

> "The navigation uses low-contrast text against the background, making the labels difficult to distinguish."

The second statement:

1. identifies a specific feature;
2. identifies a design/accessibility issue;
3. explains the effect on the user.

Use this pattern:

**OBSERVATION → PRINCIPLE → USER IMPACT**

For example:

> The main navigation uses small text with limited contrast against the background (**observation**). This weakens contrast and readability (**principle**), making navigation options more difficult to identify quickly (**user impact**).

A strong evaluation can then add:

**RECOMMENDATION**

> Increase the contrast between the navigation text and background and ensure the text remains clearly readable across viewport sizes.

The complete evaluation becomes:

**Observe → Connect → Explain → Recommend**

# Academic Integrity

Workshop 1 evaluates **your ability to observe, evaluate, explain, and recommend**.

You may discuss general usability, accessibility, and design concepts with classmates, but your submitted evaluation must represent your own analysis.

Do not:

* copy another student's findings;
* copy online website reviews;
* reuse another person's screenshots and present them as your observations;
* submit AI-generated evaluation statements without independently verifying the claims against the actual website.

If you use an AI tool to help clarify terminology or improve your writing, you remain responsible for inspecting the website yourself and verifying every claim.

A statement that sounds convincing isn't evidence that the statement is correct.
